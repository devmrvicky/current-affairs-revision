# PASSAGE 1
## Space Technology and the Purpose of Public Institutions

India's space programme has entered a new phase. For decades, the country's space agency was primarily associated with launching satellites and developing technologies under considerable financial and technological constraints. Today, however, space technology has become closely connected with communication, weather forecasting, disaster management, agriculture and national security. This expansion of applications has naturally increased expectations from India's space institutions.

Yet, greater expectations should not mean that every possible activity must be pursued by the same institution. A public organisation such as ISRO has a distinctive role in building capabilities that may not immediately attract private investment but can produce substantial benefits for society. Its strength lies not merely in launching rockets but also in developing technologies, creating scientific expertise and addressing problems that have wider national importance.

The growing participation of private companies in the space sector is therefore not necessarily a threat to public institutions. On the contrary, it can allow the government space programme to concentrate on areas requiring long-term research and strategic attention, while private enterprises take up commercially viable activities. Such a division, however, requires a clear policy framework. Without it, competition between institutions may create confusion rather than efficiency.

The challenge, therefore, is not to choose between the public and private sectors, but to determine what each can do best. India needs an ecosystem in which public institutions provide scientific leadership and technological foundations, while private companies contribute innovation, investment and speed. If this balance is maintained, the expansion of the space sector can serve not only commercial interests but also broader developmental goals.

# PASSAGE 2
## When Cities Become Hotter Than Their Surroundings

A city is not merely a collection of buildings and roads. The materials from which it is constructed can fundamentally alter its local climate. Concrete, asphalt and other dense surfaces absorb and retain heat during the day, releasing it slowly after sunset. At the same time, the replacement of vegetation with buildings reduces the natural cooling that occurs when plants release water into the atmosphere. The result is what scientists describe as the *urban heat island* effect: cities can remain considerably warmer than nearby rural areas, particularly at night.

This phenomenon is not simply an environmental curiosity. Persistent nighttime heat can prevent the human body from recovering from daytime exposure, especially during prolonged periods of extreme weather. The burden is not distributed equally either. People living in poorly ventilated homes, working outdoors, or lacking access to reliable cooling may face substantially greater risks than those who can afford air-conditioning and better-insulated housing.

Urban planning, therefore, has consequences that extend beyond aesthetics. Planting trees, creating shaded public spaces and protecting existing vegetation can reduce exposure to heat. Yet such measures should not be treated as universal solutions. Trees require water and maintenance, while poorly planned planting can sometimes obstruct airflow or place additional pressure on scarce resources. Similarly, air-conditioning can protect individuals indoors but, when widely used, increases electricity demand and may contribute to outdoor heat through the release of waste heat.

The more difficult task is consequently to design cities in which protection from heat does not depend entirely on an individual's ability to purchase it. This requires combining several relatively modest interventions—better building design, reflective surfaces, vegetation, accessible public spaces and early warnings during extreme heat. No single measure is likely to eliminate the problem. The objective should instead be to reduce vulnerability across the urban population.

A heat-resilient city, in this sense, is not necessarily one that never becomes hot. It is one in which high temperatures are less likely to become a serious threat to people's health and livelihoods.

# ⭐ MOST IMPORTANT SSC LESSON FROM THESE TWO PASSAGES

## 1. Do not over-infer

A passage may say:

> “Trees can reduce exposure to heat.”

But that does **not** mean:

> “Trees can eliminate urban heat.”

The second statement is much stronger.

### Watch for extreme words:
**always, never, completely, entirely, only, all, none, eliminate, must**

If the passage is moderate but an option is absolute, examine that option very carefully.

---

# 2. “Related” does not mean “Correct”

An option can contain words from the passage and still be wrong.

SSC often creates an option that is **partially supported** but changes the author's meaning.

Ask yourself:

> **“क्या passage वास्तव में इतना ही कह रहा है?”**

If the option goes beyond the passage, reject it.
